Has the price actually moved?

The median sale barely shifts across sixteen years — but that is the £550,000 floor at work, not the market. As prices rise, ever more modest houses cross the threshold and drag the observed median back down. The honest measure is the same house sold twice, which holds size, plot and street constant. Both lines are set to 100 in 2013; the gap between them is the illusion.

Source: HM Land Registry Price Paid Data, filtered to sales of £550,000 and over completed between 1 July 2010 and 22 June 2026. Of the 11,460 records in that extract, 2,496 are set aside before anything is counted and 8,964 sales of a single dwelling remain: 2,454 are not homes at all but commercial, agricultural or mixed-use property typed “Other” — the £111m Butlins site at Skegness among them; 19 are one property split across two titles at a single price, such as a farmhouse sold with its cottage; 16 are duplicates of another record; and 7 are priced beyond any verified sale of their own property type, which makes them land or an apportioned portfolio price rather than a house — an “£8.5m terraced house” in Melton Mowbray, for instance. The categories are mutually exclusive, so they sum exactly to 2,496.

The towns and cities on the map are orientation markers, not data — each one is checked at build time to fall inside the postcode district it belongs to.

Caveats worth holding on to: the £550k floor means every median is a top-end median; Price Paid excludes most new-build plot sales made under option agreements and any transfer not lodged for registration; 2026 covers January to June only and is marked as partial throughout; three postcode districts (DN17, LE67 and the non-geographic NG80) drop out entirely because every £550k+ record they held was non-residential. Land Registry category B sales — repossessions, buy-to-lets and purchases by companies — are kept, because category B means an unverified entry rather than a below-market price, and these sit on the same price distribution as the verified ones.
